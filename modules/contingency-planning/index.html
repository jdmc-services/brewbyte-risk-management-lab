2. Build the contingency planning program in seven steps

Contingency planning (CP) is the coordinated process of preparing for, detecting, responding to, and recovering from disruptions. It begins with leadership authorization and continues through tests and updates.

  1. Develop the CP policy statement. Leadership establishes scope, authority, responsibilities, and support. BrewByte: Who may activate emergency procedures across all stores?
  2. Conduct the BIA. Identify critical processes, dependencies, impact, RTO, RPO, and maximum tolerable downtime. BrewByte: Can payment processing be unavailable for four hours?
  3. Identify preventive controls. Reduce the probability and impact of events. BrewByte: MFA, segmentation, patching, least privilege, tested backups.
  4. Create contingency strategies. Select workable alternatives. BrewByte: Cloud failover, offline order forms, secondary ISP, warm site.
  5. Develop the contingency plan. Write the IR, DR, and BC plans, owners, contacts, escalation, and before/during/after procedures.
  6. Test, train, and exercise. Desk checks, structured walkthroughs, simulations, and carefully controlled full-interruption tests reveal gaps before a real event.
  7. Maintain the plan. Update contacts, dependencies, cloud providers, staff roles, and recovery procedures after growth, incidents, and tests.

Who does what?

CP team

Coordinates planning, policy, BIA, dependencies, and testing.

IR team / CIRT (CSIRT)

Classifies events, contains threats, preserves evidence, and coordinates cybersecurity response.

DR team

Restores infrastructure, systems, applications, and validated data.

BC team

Keeps essential business processes operating through alternate workflows and facilities.

BRP: Business resumption planning often combines closely related DR and BC recovery activities. Crisis management: Leadership coordinates high-impact decisions and communications during a disaster.

Before, during, after: Before = prevention, preparation, training. During = detect, classify, communicate, contain, continue essential services. After = eradicate causes, restore and validate, monitor, conduct an AAR.

3. Key terms: what they mean and why BrewByte needs them

Expand each term. Connect its definition to a decision that a manager, help-desk analyst, incident responder, or recovery specialist must make.

Adverse event

Any occurrence that negatively affects operations, information, safety, or services.

BrewByte example: A power failure interrupts all BrewByte stores; teams respond to impact before confirming cause.

After-action review (AAR)

A documented, blame-free review of what happened, what worked, and what should change.

BrewByte example: Compare help-desk tickets, alert timestamps, and restoration records.

Alert message

A notification that a possible problem or incident requires attention.

BrewByte example: A monitoring tool reports unusual database activity.

Alert roster

The maintained list of people and contacts to notify during an event.

BrewByte example: Store manager, help desk, CIRT lead, cloud provider, executive sponsor.

Apprehend and prosecute

An incident-handling philosophy that includes identifying an attacker and preserving evidence for possible legal action.

BrewByte example: Preserve forensic images and chain of custody before restoring a compromised laptop.

Protect and forget

A recovery-focused philosophy emphasizing repair and prevention rather than identifying or prosecuting an attacker.

BrewByte example: Restore service and fix vulnerabilities; still preserve evidence when required by policy or law.

Business continuity (BC)

Keeping essential business processes functioning despite disruption.

BrewByte example: Use a documented manual order process while the online ordering app is down.

Business impact analysis (BIA)

Analyzing disruption consequences and establishing recovery priorities and tolerances.

BrewByte example: Determine acceptable payment downtime and dependencies.

Business resumption planning (BRP)

A combined or coordinated approach to restoring technology and resuming business operations.

BrewByte example: Rebuild payment systems and reconcile transactions before declaring operations normal.

Cold site

An alternate facility with space and basic utilities but little or no ready-to-run IT infrastructure.

BrewByte example: Lower cost but longer setup and restoration time.

Warm site

An alternate facility with some infrastructure installed but requiring additional configuration or data restoration.

BrewByte example: Middle-ground cost and recovery speed.

Hot site

An alternate site with near-ready systems and connectivity for rapid recovery.

BrewByte example: Fast recovery but typically higher cost.

Contingency planning (CP)

The overarching process for preparing for, responding to, and recovering from disruptive events.

BrewByte example: Unites BIA, IR, DR, BC, tests, policy, and maintenance.

Crisis management (CM)

Leadership-level coordination and communication during major incidents or disasters.

BrewByte example: Executives decide on store closures, public updates, and emergency funding.

Cybersecurity incident response team (CIRT / CSIRT)

A designated team responsible for investigating and handling cybersecurity incidents.

BrewByte example: Security analyst, network engineer, legal liaison, and service owner.

Database shadowing

Maintaining a synchronized copy of a database, often at another location, to support availability and recovery.

BrewByte example: A replicated ordering database can reduce interruption; corrupted data may also replicate.

Desk check

A plan test in which a responsible person reviews a procedure for completeness and accuracy without executing it.

BrewByte example: Confirm the help-desk escalation roster and backup restore instructions are current.

Structured walkthrough

A group discussion of plan steps and responsibilities, without actual production interruption.

BrewByte example: Team members talk through a simulated POS outage.

Simulation

An exercise that imitates conditions and decisions of an incident without a real disruptive event.

BrewByte example: Practice alerting, triage, and communications using fictional data.

Full-interruption test

A high-impact exercise that actually interrupts or transfers operations; requires careful approval and safeguards.

BrewByte example: Move a noncritical test service to an alternate recovery environment.

Incident classification

Evaluating an incident candidate to determine whether an actual incident occurred.

BrewByte example: Categorize a database alert as possible, probable, or definite based on evidence.

Possible / probable / definite indicators

Increasing confidence levels for suspected incidents, based on corroborating evidence.

BrewByte example: Unusual login → multiple correlated alerts → confirmed unauthorized export.

Digital forensics

Identification, preservation, acquisition, analysis, and reporting of digital evidence.

BrewByte example: Examine a verified forensic image rather than modifying the suspect disk.

RTO / RPO / WRT / MTD

RTO targets restoration time; RPO targets tolerable data loss; WRT covers additional work recovery; MTD limits overall process outage.

BrewByte example: RTO 2h + WRT 1h must fit inside MTD 4h; RPO 15m is separate.

Data at rest

Data stored on disks, databases, or backups.

BrewByte example: Encrypt database storage and control backup access.

Data in transit

Data moving across networks between endpoints or services.

BrewByte example: Use TLS for customer orders traveling from browser to cloud app.

Data in use

Data actively processed in memory or by applications.

BrewByte example: Restrict processes, user access, and sensitive information exposure.

4. Protect the network, applications, and data throughout their lifecycle

Cloud services can improve availability through managed backups, replication, and geographic redundancy, but cloud adoption does not eliminate risk. BrewByte must define shared responsibilities, retention, access control, encryption, and tested recovery.

Data at rest

Stored customer records, payroll files, database snapshots, and backups.

Controls: encryption, access control, immutable/offline backup where appropriate, retention and restore testing.

Data in transit

Orders, payroll transfers, APIs, and cloud synchronization traveling over networks.

Controls: TLS, secure VPN or equivalent access, authenticated APIs, network segmentation, certificate management.

Data in use

Customer information actively processed by the POS, payroll app, or cloud database.

Controls: least privilege, endpoint hardening, application security, process isolation, monitoring, and minimizing sensitive data exposure.

How the help desk supports incident response

  1. Employee reports suspicious pop-up or payment outage through a ticket or phone call.
  2. Help desk records time, location, affected asset, observed symptoms, and business impact; it avoids altering potential evidence.
  3. Analyst checks monitoring alerts and correlates multiple store reports; escalates using the alert roster.
  4. CIRT classifies the incident, authorizes containment, preserves logs and forensic evidence, and communicates status.
  5. DR validates backups and restores affected technology; BC keeps stores operating through approved alternate processes.
  6. After recovery, ticket timeline and lessons learned inform the AAR and knowledge base.
Important: A cloud database replica is not the same as a protected backup. Deletions or corruption can replicate too. Recovery needs tested point-in-time restore capability and clear RPO/RTO targets.

6. Reinforcement: the full contingency planning picture

Why leadership matters

IT and cybersecurity managers build the plans, but business leaders authorize priorities, resources, and risk acceptance.

Four CP components

BIA informs IR, DR, and BC. They may be integrated into one plan or coordinated through linked procedures.

Five triggers for an actual incident

Loss of availability, integrity, or confidentiality; violation of policy; or violation of law. Classification relies on evidence and organizational criteria.

Respond to impact, not labels

Do not delay while arguing whether an outage is an incident, a disaster, or an operational failure. Escalate and transfer ownership when evidence clarifies cause.

Recover safely

Assess damage; close vulnerabilities; improve safeguards and monitoring; restore validated data, services, and processes; monitor for recurrence.

Test and improve

Desk check → structured walkthrough → simulation → approved full-interruption test. Finish major incidents with an AAR and plan updates.

Scenario-based team discussion

At 8:30 a.m., the cloud ordering service fails at all stores, one location loses internet connectivity, and payroll reports an unexpected login. The cause is unknown. Which events should become help-desk tickets? Who gets an alert message? Which events might qualify as incidents? What are the BC workarounds? Which systems need forensic preservation? How do you prioritize by BIA rather than assumptions?

Teaching distinction: IR contains and investigates security events; DR restores technology; BC sustains business services; crisis management coordinates high-impact leadership decisions. All should share one reliable timeline and alert roster.

7. Apply the four pillars of contingency planning

Business impact analysis (BIA)

Assumes a disruption occurred. Determines critical processes, dependencies, tolerable losses, and recovery priorities.

Incident response (IR)

Detects, analyzes, contains, eradicates, and recovers from security incidents while documenting evidence and decisions.

Disaster recovery (DR)

Restores damaged systems, infrastructure, and technology services after major disruptions.

Business continuity (BC)

Keeps essential business processes operating using workarounds, alternate facilities, or alternate service arrangements.

Policy versus plan versus procedure

Policy: leadership mandates recovery priorities and assigns authority. Plan: the organization identifies the response and recovery approach. Procedure: a team follows documented, testable steps to restore a particular service.

Discuss: A storm damages BrewByte's primary server room. Is this automatically a cybersecurity attack? No. The response is driven by the impact and the organization's activation criteria, even when the cause is initially unknown.

8. Interactive BIA: Can BrewByte meet its recovery target?

RPO = maximum acceptable amount of data loss, measured in time. RTO = targeted system restoration time. WRT = additional time to resume usable business operations. MTD = maximum tolerable downtime for the business process.

Teaching model: total process restoration time = RTO + WRT. RPO is a separate data-loss constraint and is not added to downtime.
