Open-source intelligence (OSINT) is the systematic collection, validation, and analysis of lawfully accessible information to answer a defined intelligence question. Publicly discoverable does not mean harmless or authorized for exploitation.

03 / Match the security management model

Choose the model that best explains each control objective. This is a conceptual exercise: a real organization may use multiple complementary models.

Learn the vocabulary before making decisions

Read each model as a rule for protecting a different business property. All names, people, and events are fictional.

Bell–LaPadula — protect confidentiality

Confidentiality: preventing unauthorized disclosure. Subject: a user or process requesting access. Object: a protected file or record. Security classification: sensitivity level assigned to information. Clearance: authorization level assigned to a subject.

No read up: a lower-clearance user cannot read higher-classified information. No write down: a higher-level subject cannot write protected information to a lower-classified destination (in the strict model).

BrewByte analogy: The CEO may read a confidential acquisition plan; a cashier cannot. The CEO also must not copy the confidential plan into a public store bulletin.

Remember: secrets stay at their authorized level. This is a formal model; real access decisions also depend on need-to-know and organizational policy.

Biba — protect integrity

Integrity: accuracy, completeness, and trustworthiness of information. Integrity level: degree of trust assigned to data or a subject. Contamination: untrusted input affecting trusted information.

No read down: a high-integrity subject should not read lower-integrity information in the strict model. No write up: a low-integrity subject cannot modify higher-integrity data.

BrewByte analogy: A customer comment or unverified supplier spreadsheet must not directly change BrewByte’s approved POS pricing database. An authorized employee validates it first.

Remember: untrusted data must not corrupt trusted records. Real workflows may safely ingest lower-trust data through validation and controlled interfaces.

Clark–Wilson — protect business transactions

Well-formed transaction: a change performed through an approved procedure. Separation of duties: dividing sensitive steps among different authorized people. Constrained Data Item (CDI): protected business data whose integrity is controlled. Unconstrained Data Item (UDI): input not yet governed by those integrity constraints. Transformation Procedure (TP): authorized process that changes CDIs. Integrity Verification Procedure (IVP): check that CDIs satisfy integrity rules.

BrewByte analogy: A cashier requests a large refund, a manager approves it, the POS executes the approved transaction, and an audit record is retained. The cashier cannot directly edit the sales ledger.

Remember: trusted outcomes require approved steps, checks, and accountability.

04 / Build BrewByte's management approach

The board approves a cybersecurity program but asks you to choose an organizing framework and operating rules. The company has a small IT team and an external managed security provider.

First distinguish these terms

Reference model: a conceptual structure used to explain, compare, or guide a security design. Methodology: an organized way to carry out work. Framework: a structured set of outcomes, practices, or processes for managing a domain. Standard: an agreed set of requirements or specifications. Architecture: how security components and controls fit together in an actual environment.

NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF 2.0)

Free, outcome-oriented framework organized around Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, and Recover. BrewByte can use it to prioritize its cybersecurity program.

COBIT (ISACA)

Enterprise governance and management framework for information and technology. Useful for decision rights, performance, oversight, and alignment with business objectives. Full resources and some supporting materials may require licensing.

ISO/IEC 27001 and 27002

International information security management and control standards. The standards generally require purchase; BrewByte could use them to structure an ISMS and control practices.

Common Criteria (ISO/IEC 15408)

Product security evaluation criteria; not a substitute for an enterprise cybersecurity program. TCSEC and ITSEC help explain the historical development of evaluation approaches.

How to choose: Test each candidate against BrewByte’s size, risk profile, resources, and need for flexibility, scalability, robustness, and useful detail. Formal models may inform policies, software controls, hardware design, or combinations; they rarely become a complete operational implementation by themselves.

Access control: four steps

Identification

“I am BrewByte cashier 104.”

Authentication

Prove identity using approved credentials and MFA.

Authorization

Permit sales entry but not payroll changes.

Accountability

Record who performed the action, when, and with what outcome.

Least privilege: minimum permissions for a role. Need-to-know: access only to specific information required for assigned work. Separation of duties: split sensitive transactions among different authorized people.
